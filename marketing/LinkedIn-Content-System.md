## How this works (read once)

You're behind because the hard part isn't posting — it's deciding what to say and writing it well. This system removes that. Every week you get **2 finished, ready-to-paste posts**:

- **1 personal post** (your founder voice — opinionated, build-in-public)
- **1 Mori company-page post** (product + food value)

You read them, tweak a word if you want, paste, post. That's it. A standing weekly task (set up separately) regenerates fresh drafts each Monday so you never open a blank page again.

**Why manual posting (not full automation):** LinkedIn's API doesn't allow clean hands-off auto-posting for personal or company pages, and browser-automation of LinkedIn risks your accounts. The leverage is in the writing, not the clicking — so we automate the writing and you keep a 30-second paste step. If you later connect a scheduler (Buffer, Taplio, Hootsuite), these drafts drop straight into it.

## WEEK 1

### 🟦 Personal — Tuesday (the decision)

I just cut a feature my users explicitly asked for.

A "lifetime" plan. One payment, app forever. People love it. It's the single most-requested pricing option for any indie app.

I killed it before it shipped.

Here's the math nobody tells you: AI-powered apps have a cost floor that never goes away. Every recipe my app personalizes, every meal plan it generates — that's a real bill, every month, for the life of that user. A one-time payment against a forever cost is a slow bleed dressed up as a win.

Lifetime deals work when your marginal cost trends to zero. Mine doesn't. So a lifetime plan would mean my best, most-loyal early users quietly become my least profitable ones. That's backwards.

Building solo teaches you to respect your own unit economics before you fall in love with a conversion bump.

Monthly and annual it is. Less exciting. Still here in three years.

## WEEK 2

### 🟦 Personal — Tuesday (the hot take)

A meal-planning app I respected shut down last summer. It had funding, a real team, years in market.

Most people read that as "meal planning is a hard business."

I read it as the opposite.

The category didn't fail — the *format* did. Fixed menus. Here's your week, same as everyone else's week, lightly filtered by a diet tag. It's a magazine, not a product. The second it stops matching your actual taste, you're back to staring into the fridge.

The thing no one had cracked: planning that's personalized to how *you* eat, learned passively, not configured in a settings screen. That white space didn't close when that company died. It opened.

Hot take: in consumer AI, the winners won't be the apps with the most features. They'll be the ones that quietly learn you and disappear into your routine. "It just knows" beats "look what it can do."

That's the bet I'm making.

The average household throws out roughly a third of the food it buys. Most of it dies in the back of the fridge after one use.

## WEEK 3

### 🟦 Personal — Tuesday (the lesson)

A bug that taught me more about shipping than any feature ever did:

My app's recipe images were loading slowly. Classic. I'd already compressed them, slimmed the database queries, done everything the checklist says.

Still slow.

The real culprit wasn't the images at all. It was *how many loaded at once* — five horizontal rows, every card rendering on screen at the same moment, ~50 image requests racing on the first paint. The phone's network couldn't fan that wide, so everything queued behind everything else.

The fix wasn't "make the images smaller." It was "stop asking for all of them at once."

The lesson I keep relearning solo: the slow thing is rarely the thing you're staring at. Performance bugs hide one layer up from where the pain shows. Zoom out before you optimize.

Measure the system, not the symptom.

#engineering #buildinpublic #mobiledev #startups

### 🟩 Mori — Thursday (behind the catalog)

A quiet truth about most recipe apps: nobody tested the recipes.

They're scraped, auto-imported, or generated and shipped. The macros are guesses. The steps skip the part where it actually gets hard. The portions serve "4" but really serve 2.5.

We went the other way. Every one of Mori's 2,600+ recipes has been put through a quality pass: real steps in real order, macros grounded in USDA data (always labeled estimated), sane portions, and a photo. When a recipe says 30 minutes, we want it to mean 30 minutes.

It's slower to build a catalog this way. It's also the whole point — a personalized plan is only as good as the recipes underneath it.

Curated beats scraped. Every time.

## WEEK 4

### 🟦 Personal — Tuesday (the number / hard week)

Shipping solo means the version number lies.

My app's store listing says "2.0." The actual build number is 6.1. There's no 3, 4, or 5 in between that users ever saw — they're rejected submissions, pulled releases, and one train I had to abandon because the App Store won't accept a version lower than what's already live.

Every one of those gaps is a week I thought I was done and wasn't.

I used to find this demoralizing — all that work, invisible. Now I read the gap between the marketing number and the build number as the actual resume. "2.0" is the story customers need. "6.1" is the truth: dozens of attempts, most of them quiet failures, to make the story true.

If you're building alone and your version numbers don't match your scars — good. That gap is the work.

#buildinpublic #solofounder #startups #appstore
