## 1. Personal post (Jacob — founder voice) → post Tuesday morning

I wrote a rule this month that could kill my own launch date.

The feature I've spent the most time on — an engine that plans your whole week of dinners from the recipes you've swiped — only ships if it clears one number: at least 40% of the meals it auto-plans have to actually get cooked during a week of real use. Miss it, no submission.

That feels backwards for a solo founder. Every instinct says ship it, get it in front of people, iterate live. But a meal planner that suggests food you don't make is just a prettier version of the blank-page problem I built Mori to solve. "Looks smart in a demo" isn't the bar. "You actually cooked it" is.

So I'm dogfooding my own dinners for a week before I let myself believe the screenshots. If it clears the gate, great. If it doesn't, the honest move is a smaller, humbler paid tier — not dressing up a guess as a flagship.

Building in public means showing the gates, not just the wins.

## 2. Mori company-page post → post Thursday morning

A third of what the average household buys ends up in the bin. Most of it spoils because nobody planned around it.

Mori runs the other direction. You swipe on recipes you actually want to eat, Mori learns your taste, and when something's about to spoil in your fridge it nudges recipes that use it up first — then sends the whole list to Instacart in a couple of taps.

Behind it: 2,600+ recipes, every one with real steps, estimated macros, and dietary filters that actually hold — vegetarian, vegan, pescatarian are hard filters, not polite suggestions. No staring into the fridge at 6pm. No "what do you want for dinner" text thread that goes nowhere.

That's the whole idea — you never decide what's for dinner again. Mori learns your taste, plans your week, and ships the groceries.

Free on the US and Canada App Store.
